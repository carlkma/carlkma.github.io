Madame Bovary - In search for perpetual novelty

I started reading Flaubert’s Madame Bovary with one simple objective. Madame Bovary’s (Emma’s) unrealistic dreams lead to her eventual downfall, but could I comprehend why Emma thinks and acts the way she does? Since this is the first blog post related to my readings, here are some disclaimers. I will not be providing a summary of the text as plenty of people (or AI) write better summaries than I do. My analysis will most certainly be limited, compared to people who are well versed or well trained for literary analysis. Instead, I will write about the text in general, and how it relates to my personal experience. If you want to learn about the book, this is not the right place. Stay only if you want to learn more about me, how I think, and how reading has inspired me to think differently. The first instance where I could resonate with Emma is her love, or obsession rather, of Paris. Learning about Paris through books and journals, the city is “vague” but “glimmered before Emma’s eyes”. The vagueness does not come from Emma’s lack of understanding of the city. In fact, she clearly identifies the “anguish hidden beneath smiles” or the absurdity of “[riding] horses to death at pleasure parties”. Paris is far from perfect – full of contradictions – yet Emma turns a blind eye to the unpleasant and allows herself to dwell in her fantasies. The vagueness of Paris in Emma’s mind is intentional. To love Paris, Emma would have to weigh the contradictions; but to be obsessed with Paris, a vague, ideal picture suffices.
She confused in her desire the sensualities of luxury with the delights of the heart, elegance of manners with delicacy of sentiment.
I have been to Paris, and frankly, have been confused like Emma. The luxury (of food, attractions, merchandise) is delightful, and it makes me say: “Oh I love Paris”. In saying that, I choose to forget about the less pleasant things in Paris like pickpocketing or the lack of public toilets (quite infuriating to tourists indeed). I would go so far as to criticize my hometown of Toronto, claiming that Toronto is an average, boring city. This is no different than Emma’s opinion of her “wearisome” hometown in the countryside and the “mediocrity of existence”. The “elegance of manners” is another interesting topic. I experience that not in Paris (I don’t speak French) but more so through the hospitality sector in other big cities like London and Hong Kong. People greet you by your family name, park your car, hold the door for you, and welcome you with the warmest and kindest words. Are these people sincere? Do they treat you with manners because they like you or because they have to? In hospitality, it is almost certainly the latter case. While Parisian high society may be different – I would not know – I suppose there may be some parallels. Many people would act in great manners, but only few people are acting genuinely. To see through this layer of phoniness makes it painful, and I am convinced that Emma is consciously deceiving herself as she dreams about Paris. It is self-deception that drives Emma to spend more than she can afford. It is self-deception that drives Emma into adulterous relationships, first with Rodolphe and then with Leon. Emma and Rodolphe’s relationship peaks during the agricultural show, where they become intimate in the backdrop of speeches and noisy crowds. The noise in the background shuts down rationality, leaving both “intoxicated” by love and desire. Weeks later, when the noise dies down, their love seems to “lessen beneath [Emma] like the water of a stream absorbed into its channel”. The water of love is continuously flowing out, emptying itself, drying up the relationship; Emma could see it but “would not believe” it. I make no conjecture on whether Emma has initially deceived herself, thinking that adultery could earn her perpetual excitement and novelty. Perhaps there is no self-deception. Perhaps Emma is a victim of naivety. However, fully aware that her passionate relationship with Rodolphe quickly deteriorates into one like a married couple, she repeats it with Leon. Emma is unwilling to learn her lesson. The ending? Not too different: “Emma found again in adultery all the platitudes of marriage”. Emma’s obsession with Paris and her rejection of life in the countryside foreshadow her adulterous relationships. To understand this, I will go back to my own Toronto/Paris analogy. I truly love Paris from my past travels. I would love to “break up” with Toronto and move to Paris, but friends and colleagues have warned me that if I were to live and work there, I may soon discover problems – language difficulties, differences in lifestyle, or even discrimination. Yes, if I fully accept the challenges of living in Paris, I will enjoy Paris as a resident as much as I did as a tourist. However, if I move there without an understanding of these potential problems, I might soon have to “break up” with Paris in search for somewhere else. To truly love a city (or likewise, a person), one cannot turn a blind eye to its weaknesses. Emma fails to look under the pretentious façade of Paris, as she similarly overlooks the unsustainable nature of her relationships with Rodolphe and Leon. Fell blindly for Paris, it is in Emma’s character that she inevitably falls into adultery. Emma’s character is a static one which guaranteed her downfall, but Flaubert skillfully paints her as round character worthy of debate. There still seems to be a layer of mist preventing me from fully understanding Madame Bovary. I look forward to rereading the book, but up next: Anna Karenina. Perhaps reading Anna Karenina could provide me with more insights for Madame Bovary.
